# Viktor Ivanovych Polozhiy

![Viktor Ivanovych Polozhiy](../assets/images/polozhiy.jpg)

**Birth:** August 11, 1949, Staroziniv, Bilopillia Raion, Sumy Oblast, Ukrainian SSR  
**Death:** May 21, 2004, Ukraine  
**Occupation:** Writer, literary critic, editor, educator  
**Languages:** Ukrainian, Russian  
**Notable Works:** *Ashes on Wounds*, *The Sunny Wind*, *The City That Never Was*, *Eternal City*  
**Affiliations:** Member of the Union of Soviet Writers, Member of the Union of Ukrainian Writers  

## Biography

### Early Life and Education  
Viktor Polozhiy was born in **Staroziniv, Bilopillia Raion, Sumy Oblast**, into a family headed by a collective-farm chairman — a household his memoirs recall as one of complicity with the Soviet apparatus and simultaneous private cynicism toward it. He studied at the **Philology Faculty of Taras Shevchenko National University of Kyiv**.

After university he worked as a **teacher and later school director** in **Volyn Oblast** — the Polissia experience behind *Once There Lived Ivan*. In **1983** he completed **postgraduate studies at the Institute of Literature of the Academy of Sciences of the Ukrainian SSR**, where he later worked as a **researcher**. His dissertation on **Yuri Bondarev** was published as a monograph.

### Literary Career  
Polozhiy debuted in **science fiction** with *Something is Wrong...* (1980), gaining recognition with the novel *Ashes on Wounds* (1981).

He became known for his **masculine, rigorous narrative style**, as seen in *Ashes on Wounds*, *Stratification*, *The Ball*, and *Once Upon a Time There Was Ivan*. His writing was **highly acclaimed in Moscow**, earning multiple literary awards.

Apart from fiction, he was a **prominent literary critic** and served as **editor of the prose department** at the journal *Kyiv*, **chief editor of the Dovzhenko Film Studio** (from 1985), and editor of the film association "Zemlya". He compiled the science fiction anthologies *ППФ-80* and *PFF-84*, and reviewed for *PFF-86*. His critical survey *A Glance from the Past* (1986) places Ukrainian writing inside a genealogy running from Verne through Wells and Čapek, and quotes Lem on how "already-formed technology presses upon existing culture and seems to force people actively to submit to the needs of technical processes."

An amusing detail from his own memoirs: Polozhiy had originally set his sights on a technical education and twice failed to gain admission to the Moscow Engineering Physics Institute before drifting into philology at Kyiv University. The examiner who gave him top marks in his foreign-language entrance exam later turned out to be **Yevhen Berezniak**, the wartime intelligence officer known as "Major Vikhr."

In his post-Soviet years Polozhiy wrote two memoir-essay collections on his generation and his years at the Dovzhenko Studio: *Запрошую до співтворчості* and *Влада мистецтва*, the latter preserving sketches of actors and directors he worked alongside, including Tarkovsky's collaborator Oleksandr Kaidanovsky. Their closing diagnosis is generational: a "generation of unrealized personalities" shaped by decades of enforced one-dimensional reading under censorship, confronted once it lifted with how much they had been kept from knowing.

### Illness and Death  
Polozhiy suffered from a **severe heart condition**, and doctors suggested **waiting for a transplant**. However, he passed away on **May 21, 2004**, at the age of **54**. He was laid to rest next to his father, Ivan Mykolayovych Polozhiy, at the **village cemetery in Yasnohorodka, Makariv Raion, Kyiv Oblast**.

## The Insider Who Wrote His Way Out

Polozhiy is an awkward fit for the standard history of Ukrainian science fiction. On paper he is a model of late-Soviet institutional success — Kyiv University philology, a dissertation on Yuriy Bondarev's officially sanctioned war prose, prose editor at *Kyiv*, chief literary editor at the Dovzhenko Studio. He said himself that he "never had problems with ideology" at university, having learned to identify the required template and apply it mechanically. Nothing in that profile predicts stories built around cybernetic theories of identity and consciousness splintering under cosmic terror.

The explanation is a structural loophole rather than a rupture. Science fiction was officially classified as a genre about technology and the future rather than socially significant present-day reality, and so functioned as a relatively unsupervised space. Censors read it for scientific plausibility and the absence of direct political allegory, and were often deaf to the philosophical register in which a story's real crisis was conducted. Walter Smyrniw's verdict on the earlier generation is that Ukrainian science fiction from the 1930s to the mid-1950s was "invariably bland and banal." Polozhiy is where that history bends.

### *Once There Lived Ivan*: the Absurd Inside the Frame

Set in a Volynian village in 1944, the novel is conventionally Soviet in architecture: a benevolent security officer, a criminalized nationalist insurgency, and a seventeen-year-old installed as acting head of the village council, handed a pistol and grenades and told to conceal his authority from his peers.

Its interior is another matter. Ivan does not choose his role; he is thrown into it, held fully responsible for choices made from a position he never selected. The moral terrain is greyer than the political frame admits — his own uncle pressures him to falsify a draft exemption; the villagers are governed by fear rather than conviction. Cornered at a farmstead by armed men who want him as their inside contact, Ivan hears the demand put plainly: "Either with the Soviets, Ivan, or with us." The novel ends mid-confrontation, as he pulls the pins on both grenades in his pocket at once.

This is a boundary situation in Karl Jaspers's sense — death, struggle and guilt arriving together, with no framework left to resolve them. Read through Camus, the grenades are neither a victory for either side nor a socialist-realist sacrifice but revolt: dignity asserted in the one moment still under Ivan's control, against what Camus called "the unreasonable silence of the world."

### *Solar Wind* (1986): the Camouflage Dropped

The collection contains no trace of Soviet ideology at all. Its opening story, **Centre of the Universe**, strands a cosmonaut severed from his tether and dramatizes his mind splitting into competing voices: "three thoughts are struggling. One philosophizes, the second stands in opposition to it, and the third he has deliberately driven into a crevice so it will not destroy the shaky calm of the first. So this is the fourth. Long live the fourth!" The self is a fragile coalition, with panic requiring a dedicated fourth voice to contain it. His consolation comes not from heroism but from Norbert Wiener, whose claim that "the individuality of the body is that of a flame rather than that of a stone" the cosmonaut converts into a promise of personal continuity. The story closes on folk fatalism — "not every bullet hits the soldier going into the attack" — equating the violence of war with the indifference of accidental death in space.

**Ships in the Forest** is set in a futuristic sanatorium for children described as "good children, only with one defect: they perceived the world as they wished to see it. The prophylactorium was to prepare them for life in the world as it is." Whether they truly met aliens is never resolved, and beneath the impressionistic surface lies an unmistakable subtext: dissidents confined to psychiatric hospitals for correction.

**Koroboro** places a pilot inside a manufactured world satisfying every criterion of the life he knows. Small inconsistencies accumulate — an engagement ring that will not turn on his fiancée's finger — until he wakes on bare stone after three subjective years to find two versions of the woman he loves. The copy belongs to the planet's shape-changing natives, and the ring has fused into her body: "Without it the combinations were disrupted; now I am Maika forever." Paul Ricoeur's distinction between *idem* (recognisable sameness) and *ipse* (selfhood able to survive change) fits exactly: the ring is an *idem*-trait borrowed from the pilot's memory, and tampering with it destroys the being's *ipse*.

**Planet with a Hole** stages one event — a beam of light drilling through a planet. Two Cold War states each accuse the other of testing a secret weapon, sever relations, introduce rationing, and abandon scientific inquiry within a day. Two seven-year-olds, left alone while their parents demonstrate, work out how to signal the beam and reach its operators: a distant research team irritated at the interruption. "Oh holy simplicity... What units of measure, what units of time... Enough! Don't interfere with our experiment." The satire has two targets: the ideological capture of knowledge, and the failure Hans Jonas named — actors capable of affecting an entire population who never let it enter their calculations.

**A World That No Longer Fits** sends an astronaut into a future where legal rights, down to whom one may marry, are set by a certified "coefficient of intellectual capacity." The card does not describe a standing that already exists; it manufactures one, converting a person into a file — Foucault's account of the examination, which "makes each individual a 'case'." The astronaut's only remaining move is to insist that reality is a dream, the last unverifiable place the apparatus cannot reach.

### One Page of Loyalty

The novella *Doctor Buatregano's Private Clinic* assembles the standard late-Soviet inventory of the capitalist world: a sun-bleached Latin American republic, a corrupt police chief, a CIA fixer, an ex-Nazi doctor running a black-site clinic. Two-thirds of the way through, the dialogue drifts without narrative pretext into a page-long digression establishing that a nurse belongs to a diaspora of Ukrainian nationalists scattered "after '45," running a jungle training camp, nursing the fantasy of retaking Ukraine "on a white horse" whose pasture nobody can locate, and serving simultaneously as death-squad mercenary and CIA informant. She can be trusted, her handler concludes, because she informs on everyone — which his employer receives as good news, since the information then "stays sealed within a narrow circle."

Every plot-relevant fact here could have been delivered in one line, and the nurse's pedigree could be swapped for any other without altering a subsequent scene. Its presence is explained by a logic external to the story: Ukrainian-language print culture was expected to renew, visibly and periodically, its condemnation of "Ukrainian bourgeois nationalism," and *Solar Wind* — set nowhere in the Soviet Union at all — was unusually exposed.

The passage reads not as revenge on the censor but as payment. One page of unmistakably correct content, delivered faintly farcically and from an unlikely mouth, purchases the latitude to spend the rest of the novella on chemical mind control, a coup manufactured via a surgically altered body double, and the dissolution of identity under duress. The generic banana-republic setting is not a failure of imagination but a structural precondition: borrowed from Soviet journalism's own stock imagery, it was pre-approved material that let Polozhiy stage state-manufactured violence under the alibi of exposing imperialism abroad.

Set beside the more literal compromises of an earlier generation — Ostap Vyshnia, **Yuriy Smolych** and others who bought their ability to publish as subscribed NKVD informants — Polozhiy's toll is textual rather than personal, a page rather than a biography. The logic is continuous: the price of authorship was a legible gesture of loyalty, sized to the risk of the work it bought.

## Legacy  
Viktor Polozhiy blended realism and science fiction, gaining recognition through translations and awards, and contributed criticism, editorial work, and screenwriting alongside his fiction. His love for literature, unique humour, and passion for football were well known among his peers.

He marks the moment the genre's official alibi — that it dealt in technology and the future rather than in socially significant present reality — stopped protecting ideological conformity and began protecting its opposite: philosophical material with no other outlet in official Ukrainian letters. *Once There Lived Ivan* shows this happening while the socialist-realist frame is still intact; *Solar Wind* shows what the genre could do once that frame was dropped.
