# Ukrainian Science Fiction Writers and Their Evolution

Ukrainian science fiction has undergone significant transformations over the past century, shaped by shifting political ideologies, technological change, and cultural movements. Read as a whole, the tradition traces a single long arc — from **techno-communism**, the early-Soviet conviction that technological progress would by itself deliver a classless utopia, through the slow collapse of that conviction, to the irony and unresolved oscillation of the present.

## The Techno-Communist Beginning

The early twentieth century saw the emergence of [Sandro Kasyanyuk](sandro-kasyanyuk.md), a Kharkiv factory worker turned writer who pioneered Ukrainian proto-science fiction with his cycle *Omachynennia Liudstva* (The Mechanization of Humanity). His stories glorify machines to the point of eroticising them, and register the sensation of early techno-communism with an immediacy no accomplished writer preserved.

[Yuriy Smolych](yuriy-smolych.md), a Kharkiv novelist and — as declassified archives revealed — a recruited secret-police informant, is conventionally credited with giving the genre its first serious multi-volume foundation in the *Beautiful Disasters* trilogy. The statesman and playwright [Volodymyr Vynnychenko](volodymyr-vynnychenko.md) staged the same dream from the opposite side: writing in exile after twice failing to hold power, his *Sun Machine* (1928) grants humanity abundance through a single invention and then asks, unsparingly, what people actually do once the dream comes true.

The 1930s brought propaganda-driven science fiction, above all in [Volodymyr Vladko](volodymyr-vladko.md)'s *Aerotorpedoes Turn Back* and *Argonauts of the Universe*, which fused Vernean technical plausibility with Wellsian future-war extrapolation into a distinctly Soviet claim: that superior Soviet science and superior Soviet social organization are the same fact. The era also saw the tragic fate of the futurist Geo Shkurupii, executed during the Stalinist purges.

## The Long Collapse

By the 1940s and 1950s the genre had narrowed into what Soviet criticism itself called "near-target" fiction. [Vasyl Berezhnyi](vasyl-berezhnyi.md)'s *To the Starry Worlds* (1956) belongs squarely to that moment — and his own career then becomes the clearest single record of techno-communism's exhaustion, ending thirty years later in the bitter irony of *The Formula of the Cosmos*, where a unifying theory of the universe is used to wrap fish.

[Ihor Rosokhovatskyi](ihor-rosokhovatskyi.md) brought a genuinely philosophical engagement with artificial minds to the genre through his cycle about "syhoms," synthetic post-human beings that reason their way past the corrupted instructions of their makers — anticipating, decades early, what is now called the AI alignment problem.

The 1960s produced two opposite exits from the same rupture. [Oles Berdnyk](oles-berdnyk.md), dissident and visionary, answered the collapse with mystical nationalism — a local, right-wing variant of hippie cosmism arrived at with almost no contact with the West. [Volodymyr Savchenko](volodymyr-savchenko.md) answered it with analytic scepticism, using *Self-Discovery* (1967) to dramatize identity, duplication, and machine agency decades ahead of Western debate. Two contemporaries, one crisis, two roads: one toward information theory and the philosophy of mind, the other toward its mystical refusal.

## After the Metanarrative

Later generations carried the genre's political engagement into new territory. [Volodymyr Zayets](volodymyr-zayets.md), a pediatrician by training, diagnosed the hollowing-out of late-Soviet hierarchies with a clinician's eye, reinventing postmodern devices without ever having read the theory. [Viktor Pologyj](viktor-pologyj.md), an insider at the Dovzhenko Film Studio, smuggled existentialist and postmodern material into ostensibly orthodox fiction — paying for the latitude, on occasion, with a single page of unmistakably correct politics.

[Yuri Shcherbak](yuri-shcherbak.md) drew on decades as physician, ecologist and ambassador to write a geopolitical dystopian trilogy whose sincerity is now its most disorienting feature. [Fedor Berezin](fedor-berezin.md)'s "future war" novels functioned less as prophecy than as rehearsal for his own later role in the war in Donbas.

Most recently, [Pavlo Ostrikov](pavlo-ostrikov.md)'s film *U Are the Universe* (2024) has brought a metamodern sensibility to Ukrainian science fiction — oscillating between irony and sincerity rather than choosing either, and refusing to say whether its hero's journey is love or futility.

## Why This History Matters

Ukrainian science fiction writers have consistently registered the political and ideological pressures of their moment, from industrial utopianism to dystopian critiques of totalitarianism. Their trajectory closely parallels the fate of positivism itself: from science as a new religion guaranteed to deliver happiness, to science as a tool whose benefit or harm depends entirely on who controls it. Their legacy is a study in how literature can both serve an ideology and, sometimes in the same book, quietly dismantle it.
